# Pool post-release contests, screen hard, cap at 20k

Only problems first published after DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B's January 2025 release can be called surely unseen. Of those, only the short-answer 2025–2026 competitions that MathArena redistributes come with enough volume, clean grading and the right difficulty for this study. The model's MathArena accuracy is **49% on AIME 2025, 68% on BRUMO 2025, 55% on SMT 2025 and 32% on HMMT Feb 2025**, which puts all four sets inside the 15–85% band ([MathArena](https://matharena.ai/models/deepseek_deepseek_distill_14b)). Rescoring MathArena's released per-sample traces at our generation cap gives **39 of those 143 problems with 1–3 of 4 samples correct at 16,384 tokens, and 41 at 20,000**. Only 20 of the 39 have a median trace of 12k tokens or less, which is what deep nested resampling needs. Four later sets have no 14B data yet: AIME 2026, HMMT Nov 2025, CMIMC 2025 and HMMT Feb 2026, 133 problems in all. Adding them should take the pool to roughly 70–80 in-band candidates before near-duplicate and memorisation exclusions. That is enough for 20 core problems plus 30–50 label problems, but with little margin. The plan below therefore screens about 260 post-release problems at 16 and then 48 samples. It sets a 20,000-token cap on the *total* trace and keeps OlymMATH-EN-EASY as a reserve stratum labelled separately. Temporal sourcing is the guarantee. Model-based contamination probes are close to random on R1-distilled models, so they can only raise red flags, never certify a problem clean. Procedurally generated puzzles make a defensible optional second source, but they are new instances of task types the model was trained on, not new tasks.

Evidence tags used throughout: **[verified]** means checked against the cited primary source by the research notes. **[computed]** means calculated for this report from MathArena's public per-sample data or from the binomial distribution. **[inference]** means our judgement. A ledger at the end separates the three and lists what could not be verified.

## "Out of distribution" means first published after January 2025, then de-duplicated

The model's weights come from two lineages, and neither publishes a clean cutoff. **The Qwen2.5-14B base was pretrained on 18T tokens, released 19 September 2024, and has no documented knowledge cutoff** ([Qwen2.5 blog](https://qwenlm.github.io/blog/qwen2.5/); [arXiv 2412.15115](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.15115)) [verified]. Its pretraining folds in Qwen2.5-Math data. That post-training data names "GSM8K, MATH, and NuminaMath" plus Chinese K-12 problems ([arXiv 2409.12122](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.12122)) [verified]. The distillation stage fine-tuned the base on **804,745 R1-generated samples, 395,285 of them maths, at an initial learning rate of 7e-5 for 2–3 epochs**. DeepSeek states that the maths SFT and RL prompts were "sourced exclusively from pre-2023 competitions" and that DeepSeek-V3's base has "a knowledge cutoff date of July 2024" ([arXiv 2501.12948v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.12948v2)) [verified]. The model card names base Qwen2.5-14B and reports 93.9% on MATH-500 and 69.7% on AIME 2024 ([HF model card](https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B)) [verified]. The R1 paper went up on arXiv on 22 January 2025 [verified]; the weights' release date of 20 January 2025 comes from memory and was not checked.

The operational boundary follows from that. **Content first published in February 2025 or later cannot be in the weights.** Content from August 2024 to January 2025 is probably absent, but nothing documents that, because Qwen's freeze date is unknown [inference]. DeepSeek itself treats AIME 2025 as its "unseen" test while conceding that "variations of test set questions or discussions of related problems may still exist on websites" in the pretraining corpus ([arXiv 2501.12948v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.12948v2)) [verified]. That concession is the crux. Temporal novelty rules out verbatim memorisation. It does not rule out a 2025 problem being a renumbered copy of a 2019 AoPS thread. **MathArena found that 8 of 30 AIME 2025 problems and 1 HMMT 2025 problem "can be found online in a similar form"**, mostly the easier ones ([arXiv 2505.23281](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.23281)) [verified]. Both labs decontaminate lexically only (Qwen with 13-gram/LCS ≥ 0.6, DeepSeek with 10-gram), so paraphrases pass straight through [verified, same sources].

For this model, treat the following as **contaminated**: GSM8K, MATH train and test (including MATH-500 and the current 20 Thought Anchors problems), NuminaMath, AIME up to 2024, AMC up to 2023, OlympiadBench, Omni-MATH, the released LiveAoPSBench-2024 split, and any AoPS problem posted before mid-2024 [inference from the lineage above]. The evidence is direct. **Qwen2.5-7B reproduces the rest of a MATH-500 problem exactly from a 60% prefix 21.2% of the time, and Qwen2.5-Math-7B 54.6%, against 3.8% for Llama-3.1-8B.** On LiveMathBench 202505 the rate falls to 0% ([arXiv 2507.10532](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.10532)) [verified]. MathArena finds most models score 10–20 points above expectation on AIME 2024 ([arXiv 2505.23281](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.23281)) [verified]. Programme data add a model-specific case: one of the current 20 problems (1591) is answered correctly 80% of the time with no reasoning at all. Treat as a **grey zone** content from August 2024 to January 2025, and pre-2025 printed sources digitised in 2025, such as OlymMATH. Both are unlikely to be memorised verbatim, but neither carries a guarantee.

## Eight post-release contests lead a ranked inventory of thirteen sources

The ranking weighs four things: how strong the temporal guarantee is, whether the problems suit deep resampling (integer answers, short traces, mid-band accuracy), measured rather than guessed 14B accuracy, and licence friction. Yield is the expected number of problems inside 15–85% under each cap. For MathArena's four 14B-evaluated sets it is computed from the released traces. A sample counts as correct at a cap only if it was correct *and* used no more output tokens than the cap. This is an exact counterfactual for MathArena's own prompt and sampling, though not for ours [computed]. For the other sets, yield is estimated from about 30% band density [inference].

### MathArena's per-sample outputs are the most useful single asset

Each `MathArena/*_outputs` dataset holds every sample's full message trace, with the fields `problem_idx`, `model_name`, `output_tokens`, `gold_answer`, `parsed_answer` and `correct` ([HF datasets-server](https://datasets-server.huggingface.co/info?dataset=MathArena/aime_2025_outputs)) [verified]. The R1-Distill-14B rows used temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95 and a 32,000-token cap. Those match DeepSeek's recommended sampling and Thought Anchors' setup ([MathArena](https://matharena.ai/models/deepseek_deepseek_distill_14b)) [verified]. The data answer the cap question directly. **At 16,384 tokens, 30% of AIME 2025 samples, 47% of HMMT Feb 2025 samples and 31% of SMT samples would be truncated. At 20,000 tokens the rates fall to 14%, 33% and 21%** [computed]. Accuracy hardly moves (AIME 2025: 49.2% at 32k, 49.2% at 20k, 46.7% at 16k), because long traces are mostly wrong anyway [computed]. Raising the cap therefore buys almost no in-band problems: 39 at 16k against 41 at 20k across the four sets. What it buys is fewer truncations, and each truncation becomes a `<NO_ANSWER>` outcome inside every resampled answer distribution [computed + inference]. These traces cannot replace our own screen, because MathArena's prompt wrapper differs from ours. They are an excellent prior for ordering and pruning the screen, and a free check that our pipeline reproduces MathArena's accuracy on the same problems [inference].

## Screen about 260 post-release problems twice to land 50–70 in band

**Pooling.** Pool all eight MathArena answer-based sets from 2025–26, 276 problems. Prune those already outside the band with high confidence. Drop problems all 8 of whose 14B and 32B samples are correct at 20k. A problem whose true rate is 0.85 does this 27% of the time, so only easy items are lost [computed]. Also drop problems where all 12 of the 14B, 32B and 70B samples are wrong. That leaves roughly 240–260 problems to screen [computed estimate]. Expected yield at a 20k cap is about 41 problems from the four measured sets and 35–45 from the four new ones. **That gives roughly 75–85 in-band candidates**, before an assumed 10–20% loss to near-duplicate and red-flag exclusions [inference]. The result, about 60–70, meets the target with little room to spare. So **screen OlymMATH-EN-EASY alongside** as a reserve stratum, tagged "curated, not temporal" and never pooled silently with the post-release problems.

**Sample sizes.** Each problem's screen estimate has binomial noise. The standard error at p = 0.5 is **0.25 with 4 samples, 0.16 with 10 (Thought Anchors' screen), 0.125 with 16 and 0.072 with 48** [computed]. Use two stages. In stage 1, draw 16 samples per problem at the study's cap and keep problems with 3–13 correct. That keeps 90% of problems with true rates of 0.3–0.7, but only 44% of those sitting exactly on the 0.15 or 0.85 edge [computed]. Losing edge problems is acceptable, because the analysis needs mid-band items. In stage 2, add 32 samples to each survivor, giving 48, and keep estimates within [0.15, 0.85]. At a rough mean of 11k output tokens, stage 1 costs about 4,000 generations (around 45M tokens) and stage 2 about 3,200 (around 35M) [inference]. Wall-clock time must come from the repository's required benchmark projection, not from this estimate. **Do not reuse screening samples to estimate accuracy from the problem start.** Selecting on noisy estimates causes regression to the mean, as the current set's median of 0.73 showed. The nested-resampling arm must re-estimate that accuracy from fresh samples [inference].

**Choosing the core 20 for deep nested resampling.** Use stricter filters. Require a 48-sample accuracy between 0.30 and 0.70, and an integer or simple rational answer. AIME takes priority; SMT's transcendental answers would need a symbolic checker inside every resample. Require a median trace of 12k tokens or less, truncation at the cap of at most 10%, and a passed manual near-duplicate review. Spread the core across at least three competitions. The constraint is real. **Only 20 of the 39 problems in band at 16k on the measured sets have a median 14B trace of 12k tokens or less: 4 from AIME 2025, 7 from BRUMO, 3 from HMMT Feb 2025 and 6 from SMT** [computed]. So the core needs AIME 2026 and HMMT Nov 2025 as well. The remaining in-band problems become the 30–50 label problems.

**Cap.** **Raise max_tokens to 20,000 and define it as a cap on the total trace (prefix plus continuation), not on each continuation.** The total must fit the 24,576-token context with the prompt; 20,000 leaves about 4.5k of headroom [computed]. Thought Anchors-style resampling regenerates from each sentence boundary. A continuation-only cap would give late prefixes a larger total budget than the trace from the problem start, which biases accuracy from late prefixes upward. A total cap keeps the comparison like for like [inference]. The 12k median filter on core problems leaves resamples from mid-trace roughly 8k tokens of room. Screening must run at exactly the cap the analysis uses, because the band is defined at that cap.

**Near-duplicate search protocol.** For each candidate, record the first-publication date and drop anything before February 2025. Then run three searches against a reference corpus: AoPS wiki and forums before 2025, Math StackExchange, NuminaMath-1.5, Omni-MATH, OlympiadBench, MATH train and test, GSM8K train, historical AIME/AMC/HMMT/SMT, and OlymMATH. The first is a lexical pass using both labs' own thresholds, 10-gram overlap and 13-gram/LCS ≥ 0.6. The second is an embedding search over the statement plus the final answer, with the top 10 matches reviewed by hand. The third is a manual web search for all core problems, as MathArena did, because no standard threshold exists for maths near-duplicates. Classify each match as *verbatim*, *same problem with different numbers*, *same key lemma* (acceptable) or *novel*. Exclude the first two from the main analysis and keep them as a labelled contamination stratum [inference, building on [arXiv 2505.23281](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.23281)]. Begin with the 8 AIME 2025 problems MathArena flagged; its appendix lists them, but the notes did not extract them.

**Contamination red-flag probes and their limits.** Run five cheap probes, and calibrate each against a **positive control**, the current 20 MATH problems including problem 1591, and a **negative control**, AIME 2026 or synthetic items. (1) No-reasoning accuracy: prefill a closed `<think></think>` block and ask for the answer directly. (2) Prefix completion on base Qwen2.5-14B, greedy, with no chat template, from 40/60/80% prefixes, scored by exact match and ROUGE-L, following the protocol of [arXiv 2507.10532](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.10532). (3) Min-K%++ on the base model, used only to rank candidates against the controls. (4) Token Probability Deviation on generated tokens, which targets distillation membership ([arXiv 2510.04850](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.04850v1)). (5) Perturbed twins with changed constants, where a falling accuracy or reuse of the old answer flags memorisation. Drop outliers on any probe. The limits are severe and must be stated in the paper. **After CoT-format contamination of R1-Distill-Qwen-7B and 14B, most detectors score AUROC ≈ 50%, and the best (LiRA) averages 58.74%** ([arXiv 2510.02386](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.02386v2)) [verified]. Detectors also miss contamination introduced through fine-tuning ([arXiv 2409.09927](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09927)) [verified]. A positive hit is informative; a negative proves nothing. If the probes cannot separate the 20 MATH problems from AIME 2026, report them as uninformative rather than as evidence of cleanliness [inference]. The no-reasoning heuristic has no published validation on R1-Distill.

**Licences.** MathArena's problem datasets are **CC BY-NC-SA 4.0** [verified via HF], and copyright in the underlying problems stays with the organisers. For AIME that is the MAA. Its reported terms require written permission for "revenue-generating purposes" and credit in the form "2017 AMC 12 B, Problem #21" ([MAA](https://maa.org/student-programs/amc/)) [unverified wording]. Non-commercial research use is compatible. The workable rules are these [inference]. Keep problem text out of the repository: store dataset ID, pinned revision, problem index and a SHA-256 of the text, and fetch at runtime. Credit every AIME item in MAA's format. Release any derived traces or labels that quote problem text under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 with attribution to MathArena and the contest. Check HMMT, SMT, CMIMC and BRUMO terms before any public release; none was found. OlymMATH (MIT), BeyondAIME (CC0) and Putnam-AXIOM (CC BY 4.0) carry no such friction.

## Puzzles make a fresh second source, but they are not a new task

Procedurally generated puzzles solve the supply problem completely: unlimited instances, tunable difficulty and exact verifiers. The catch is familiarity with the task type. **The revised R1 paper lists a 15k-question logic RL set that includes "cryptography puzzles", deductive "logic puzzles" and "arithmetic puzzles"**. The distillation data was rejection-sampled from that RL checkpoint ([arXiv 2501.12948](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12948)) [verified]. A freshly generated Knights-and-Knaves or Countdown instance is therefore **OOD by instance but not by task type**. Fine-tuning on K&K is known to produce memorisation that breaks under small perturbations ([arXiv 2410.23123](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.23123)) [verified]. The families that best suit a decision-point study are those whose traces are full of case splits. K&K with 4–7 people (generator: [mem-kk-logic](https://github.com/Unakar/Logic-RL), Apache-2.0 for Logic-RL) qualifies. So do ZebraLogic grids, whose **Z3 conflict count supplies a solver-side measure of branching**; reasoning tokens track it, and accuracy collapses beyond about 20 conflicts ([arXiv 2502.01100](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01100)) [verified]. Countdown/Mathador search qualifies too. Enigmata's Search and Sequential puzzles are probably less familiar, and **R1-Distill-32B scores only 12.4% and 18.8% on them against 62.9% on Logic** (CC BY-NC-SA) ([arXiv 2505.19914](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.19914)) [verified]. Reasoning Gym is Apache-2.0 and offers over 100 configurable generators ([arXiv 2505.24760](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24760)) [verified]. No published 14B accuracy or trace length exists for any of these generators. The plan is to generate about 200 instances across two or three families at three difficulty settings, screen them exactly like the maths problems, and report results by how familiar each task type is likely to be [inference]. One non-peer-reviewed study found anchors centred on fact retrieval rather than planning outside maths ([LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/f9cnGHCiJgo5eDkSQ/understanding-reasoning-with-thought-anchors-and-probes)). A change in anchor type is thus a plausible finding in its own right, not just noise.

The perturbation sets play a different role. **MATH-P-Hard** offers 279 hand-made Level-5 variants on which every model tested drops 10–25 points ([arXiv 2502.06453](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.06453)) [verified]. But each variant sits on top of a contaminated original, so it is the worst possible "clean" source. Its value is as a *paired contamination contrast*: run original and twin through the same pipeline and test whether anchors move. **Putnam-AXIOM Variation** has only 37 items whose answers actually change. R1-Distill-32B falls from 33% to 24% on it ([arXiv 2508.08292](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.08292v2)) [verified], and Putnam traces will strain the cap. Its generator is more valuable than its fixed items. **OlymMATH** is the strongest reserve. Its 64-sample per-problem accuracies are measured, its licence is MIT, and EASY has 67 in-band items. But "manually sourced from printed publications" is a curation guarantee, not a temporal one ([arXiv 2503.21380](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.21380)) [verified]. If one second source must be chosen, choose generated K&K plus ZebraLogic for domain contrast. Use OlymMATH only to backfill the maths count, labelled as its own stratum [inference].

## Risks and open items

The largest risk is a thin margin. The 75–85 candidate estimate rests on a single band density, about 30%, taken from four sets, and the four untested sets may come in harder; CMIMC and HMMT Feb 2026 look like it. A second risk is that near-duplicate exclusions cluster among the easy, mid-band AIME problems best suited to the core. MathArena's flagged items are "mostly easier problems" ([arXiv 2505.23281](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.23281)). Third, our prompt format will shift trace lengths away from MathArena's, so the cap rescoring must be re-run on our own screen samples. Fourth, HMMT and SMT answers include radicals, factorials, sets and transcendental constants, so the grader must handle symbolic equivalence or those items should be confined to the label set. Fifth, because the probes fail on R1 distills, residual contamination can only be bounded through design: date filters, de-duplication and paired twins. Detection cannot rule it out. Sixth, the repository's operating rules freeze the dataset and the 20 problems in `configs/mvp.yaml`. Adding problems therefore needs an amendment record and the user's approval before any GPU run. Open items: whether AIMO-3's 110 private problems were released after April 2026; whether the LiveAoPSBench pipeline can be re-run on 2025–26 posts as a further temporal source; and the licence terms of each contest organiser.

## Conclusion

The study's real constraint is trace length, not novelty. Post-release contests supply enough unseen problems in the right accuracy band, but only about half of them run short enough for deep nested resampling. And a per-continuation cap would quietly bias the accuracy estimates the analysis depends on. Defining the cap on the total trace and filtering the core by median length fixes both, at almost no cost in yield.

The contamination literature also changes what "clean" can mean in the write-up. With detectors at chance on R1 distills, the defensible claim is a design claim: dated after release, de-duplicated by a stated protocol, and contrasted with a known-contaminated control (the current 20 MATH problems). Keeping that control in the analysis, rather than discarding it, turns contamination from a confound into a measured variable. Do anchors and probe signals differ between memorised and unseen problems? That question becomes a result in its own right.
